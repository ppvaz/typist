There is a faster way to reach the coast, but I prefer the slow train. It
leaves from the small platform at the edge of town, the one with a single
bench and a timetable pinned behind cloudy plastic, and it stops at every
village along the valley. The whole journey takes three hours and ten minutes.
The express does it in ninety. Most people take the express, and I understand
why, but they miss the part of the trip that I enjoy most, which is watching
the landscape change one small step at a time.

In the first hour the train follows the river through farmland. The fields are
divided by low stone walls, and in spring the lambs run away from the noise
and then stop, confused, as the carriages pass. In the second hour the valley
narrows and the line climbs through a forest of dark pines. The air cools; you
can feel it through the window. There is a tunnel, then another, and after the
second tunnel the train comes out onto a ridge where the whole valley lies
behind you, small and green. In the third hour the land drops toward the sea,
the pines give way to gorse and heather, and the light becomes wider and
paler until, just before the last station, you catch the first gray line of
water between two hills.

On the express, that sequence is compressed into a blur. You leave the town,
you read for a while, and then you are at the coast. The destination is the
same, but the journey has no shape. On the slow train, each change arrives
separately, and there is time to notice it before the next one begins. I have
made the trip perhaps forty times, and I still find something new: a heron in
a ditch, a farmhouse with its shutters painted a different color, a path
through the pines that I have never walked but have now memorized.

I think skills are learned more like the slow train than the express. We would
like to jump straight from the first awkward attempt to easy mastery, but the
body does not work that way. It needs to pass through every village. Each stage
has its own small lessons, and skipping them leaves gaps that show up later as
hesitation or error. When I am impatient with slow progress, I remind myself of
the ridge after the second tunnel, and how much better the view is when you
have climbed to it one curve at a time.

My garden is not large. It is a narrow strip behind the house, about four
meters wide and twelve meters long, with a fence on one side and a brick wall
on the other. When we moved in, it held nothing but coarse grass, a rusted
wheelbarrow, and a rose bush that had been left alone for so long that it had
climbed halfway up the wall. I had no experience with plants, and I assumed
that a garden was something you designed once and then enjoyed. I was wrong
about that, and I am glad I was.

The first season was mostly a list of mistakes. I planted tomatoes in the
shade of the wall, where they grew tall and pale and produced three small
fruits. I sowed lettuce too thickly and then felt too guilty to thin it, so the
seedlings strangled one another. I watered in the heat of the afternoon, which
wasted half the water and scorched the leaves. By August the garden looked
tired and so did I. The only real success was the rose, which I had left
alone, and which bloomed without any help from me.

In the second year I started a notebook. Every weekend I wrote down what I had
planted, where, and when, along with the weather and anything I noticed: which
bed dried out first, where the slugs gathered, how many hours of sun reached
each corner. It was dull work, and I often wanted to skip it. But by the end of
that summer the notebook knew my garden better than I did. It showed that the
far corner received six hours of direct sun while the corner by the house
received barely two. It showed that the beans did well when I planted them a
week later than the packet suggested.

Now, in the fifth year, the garden mostly works. The tomatoes grow against the
fence where the light is strong, the lettuce is sown in short rows every two
weeks, and I water in the early morning before work. None of these changes
were clever. They were small corrections, repeated and recorded, that added up
over several seasons. People sometimes ask what my secret is, and I tell them
the truth: I pay attention, I write it down, and I try again next year. That is
less exciting than a secret, but it has the advantage of being true.

The river changes its voice with the seasons. In early spring it is loud and
brown, full of snowmelt from the hills, and it carries branches past the town
faster than a person can walk. By midsummer it has dropped to a thin, clear
murmur that slides over pale stones, and children wade across at the ford
without getting their knees wet. In autumn the leaves arrive first, a slow
parade of yellow and red, and then the rain returns and the water rises again.

I began keeping notes on the river three years ago, mostly out of curiosity.
Each morning I walked to the footbridge, looked at the painted gauge on the
old pier, and wrote the level in a small green book. I added the weather, the
color of the water, and anything unusual: a heron standing on one leg, a
broken oar caught against the pier, a line of foam that meant the mill had
opened its gates upstream. The notes were not scientific. They were simply
regular, and regular notes turn out to be surprisingly powerful.

After the first year I could see patterns that I had never noticed while
living beside the water. The level rose about two days after heavy rain in the
hills, not on the same day. A dry week in March lowered the river more than a
dry week in June, because the ground was still cold and did not hold water in
the same way. The heron came back to the same stone every morning in October,
as if it had an appointment. None of this was hidden. I had just never looked
at the river twice in the same way.

That is the quiet value of a record. Memory is generous to good days and harsh
to bad ones, so it tells a misleading story. A notebook does not care how you
felt. It says that on Tuesday the level was 41 centimeters and the water was
the color of tea, and it says the same thing a year later when you have
forgotten Tuesday entirely. Put enough of those plain entries together and a
shape appears, one that no single morning could have shown you.

Some neighbors thought the habit was odd. Why stand on a bridge in the rain to
write down a number? I understand the question, but I have stopped trying to
answer it with words. Instead I show them the green books, open to any page,
and let them read what the river was doing on their birthday.

In the last half hour before the library closes, the building changes its
mood. The afternoon crowd has gone home, the children's corner is empty, and
the only sounds are the hum of the old heating pipes and the soft thud of
returned books landing in the metal cart. A few readers remain at the long
tables near the windows. They look up now and then at the clock above the
desk, calculate how many pages they can still finish, and bend back over
their work with renewed concentration.

The librarian on the evening shift uses this time to reshelve. She pushes the
cart slowly down the aisles and slides each book into its place with a small,
precise movement of her wrist. She does not need to read the spine labels
closely anymore. After eleven years she knows the shape of the collection the
way a person knows the rooms of their own house in the dark: history is past
the second pillar, poetry is on the low shelves under the stairs, and the
large atlases lie flat on the bottom row because they are too heavy to stand.

Reshelving is not glamorous work, but a library depends on it completely. A
book in the wrong place is effectively lost. It might sit unnoticed for years,
three shelves away from where the catalog says it should be, while readers
search for it and give up. So the librarian checks her work. Every evening she
chooses one shelf at random and reads it slowly from end to end, looking for
anything out of order. Most nights she finds nothing. Now and then she finds a
single book that someone has returned to the wrong gap, and she moves it back
with quiet satisfaction.

At ten minutes to closing she walks through the reading room and says, in a low
voice, that the library will close soon. Nobody complains. The regular readers
have learned to finish a chapter, mark their place, and pack up without being
asked twice. At closing time she switches off the lights in sections, starting
at the back, so that the room darkens gradually toward the door. Then she locks
up, checks the handle twice, and walks home, leaving behind a building where
every book, as far as she knows, is exactly where it belongs.

The bicycle had been leaning against the shed for four winters when I finally
decided to repair it. The tires were flat and cracked, the chain was brown with
rust, and the brake cables had seized inside their housings. My first instinct
was to take it to a shop, but the repair shop in town had a waiting list of
three weeks, and I was curious whether I could do the work myself. I borrowed
a set of tools from a neighbor, found a manual in the library, and cleared a
space on the garage floor.

The manual was clear, but it assumed a kind of knowledge that I did not have.
It said to "remove the rear wheel," as if that were a single action. In
practice it involved shifting to the smallest gear, opening the brake, loosening
two nuts that had not moved in a decade, and working the chain off the cog
without dropping it into the dirt. The first time took me forty minutes and a
fair amount of language that I will not repeat. The second time, a week later,
took six minutes. Nothing about the bicycle had changed; my hands had simply
learned the order of the steps.

I made a habit of writing short notes as I worked. They were not elegant: "left
brake nut is 10 mm, not 9," "chain tool pin goes in from the outside," "clean
the rim before fitting new pads or they squeal." But each note saved me from
repeating a mistake, and together they turned into a personal manual more
useful than the printed one. When a friend asked me to look at her bicycle, I
realized that I could explain each step without hesitation, because I had
written down every place where I had once been confused.

The finished bicycle is not beautiful. The paint is scratched, the saddle is
mismatched, and one mudguard rattles on rough roads. But it runs smoothly, the
brakes stop firmly, and the gears change with a crisp click. More importantly,
I know how every part of it works. When something goes wrong, I no longer feel
helpless. I turn the bicycle over, look carefully, and try the simplest fix
first. Most problems, it turns out, are small ones that have been ignored for
too long, and most repairs are just patience applied in the right order.

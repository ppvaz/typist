Every summer my grandfather wrote letters from the coast. He rented the same
small cottage for three weeks each year, a stone building with a green door and
a view of the harbor, and from there he sent long letters to everyone in the
family. He wrote in pencil on lined paper, filling both sides of each sheet,
and he always began the same way: "The tide is out, the gulls are loud, and I
have nothing important to report." Then he reported everything.

He described the fishing boats leaving before dawn and returning in the early
afternoon, low in the water with their catch. He wrote about the woman who
sold fruit from a cart by the church and never gave change in coins, only in
extra plums. He noted the weather in careful detail, the way a sailor might:
wind from the southwest, light rain until ten, clearing by noon, a sharp
cold evening with stars. When I was young I skipped those parts and looked for
the stories. Now I read the weather first, because it tells me exactly what
kind of day he was living.

After he died, my mother gathered the letters into a box, sorted by year. There
are thirty-one summers in that box. Reading them in order is a strange and
moving experience. At first nothing seems to change: the boats, the fruit cart,
the green door. Then you notice small shifts. The fruit seller retires and her
daughter takes over the cart. The fishing fleet shrinks from nine boats to
five. His handwriting grows larger and less steady, and the letters become a
little shorter each year, although they never stop describing the tide.

I have started writing letters of my own, not from the coast but from ordinary
weeks at home. I try to follow his method: begin with the plain facts, write
down what I actually saw rather than what I expected to see, and do not worry
about whether anything important happened. It is harder than it sounds. My
first letters were full of opinions and empty of details. Slowly I am learning
to look first and judge later, to notice the tide before I decide what it
means. Perhaps in thirty years someone will read them in order and see the
small changes that I am too close to notice now.

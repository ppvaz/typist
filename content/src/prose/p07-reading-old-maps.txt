Old maps are honest about what their makers did not know. On a chart drawn two
centuries ago, the coastline near a busy port is traced in careful detail,
every inlet and sandbar marked with soundings in tiny numbers. Farther along,
where fewer ships had sailed, the line becomes smoother and more confident,
which is a sign that it was guessed. Farther still, the coast simply stops,
and the paper is left blank or filled with a note in faded ink: "Coast not
surveyed." I have come to like those notes more than the decorated corners.

A map is a record of attention. Wherever the surveyors spent time, the detail
is rich: hills are shaded, streams are named, and the roads bend exactly where
they bend on the ground. Wherever they hurried, the map is thin. You can read
the history of the survey in the texture of the lines, the way you can read a
person's habits in the wear on their shoes. The blank spaces are not failures.
They are simply places where the work had not yet been done.

There is a temptation, when copying an old map, to fill in the blanks. It
feels untidy to leave a coast unfinished, and a confident line looks better on
the wall. Some later mapmakers gave in to that temptation. They connected the
known points with graceful curves, added a few invented islands, and sold the
result as complete. Sailors who trusted those maps sometimes paid for it. A
guess drawn with the same ink as a measurement is more dangerous than a blank,
because it hides the difference between what is known and what is hoped.

I think about this when I look at any kind of record, including my own. It is
easy to round a number up, to remember a good week as typical, or to write
down "about the same as usual" instead of checking. Each of those small
choices smooths the line and hides a blank. A more useful habit is to mark the
unknown plainly: not measured, not checked, not sure. It looks less
impressive, but it tells the next reader, who is often your future self,
exactly where the solid ground ends.

The best maps in the museum are not the most beautiful ones. They are the ones
where you can see the surveyors thinking: a hill drawn twice and corrected, a
river marked "course uncertain," a depth given with a small question mark.
They trusted their readers with the truth, and that is why they are still worth
reading.

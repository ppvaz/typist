The bakery on the corner begins its day at four in the morning, long before
the street lamps switch off. The first person through the back door turns on
the ovens, because they need an hour to reach full heat, and then fills the
big kettle for tea. Nobody speaks until the tea is ready. After that the room
fills with a steady, practiced noise: flour poured into steel bowls, the thump
of dough turned out onto the table, the scrape of a bench knife cutting it
into equal pieces.

What surprises visitors is how little the bakers measure by eye. They weigh
everything, even the water, and they write the weights on a board by the
sink. The recipe for the country loaf has not changed in eleven years, but the
dough is different every day. It depends on the temperature of the room, the
humidity, the age of the flour, and the mood of the starter that lives in a
covered jar on the top shelf. So the bakers adjust. On a cold morning they use
warmer water; on a humid one they hold back a little of it. They make these
changes calmly, because they have seen the same problems hundreds of times.

Shaping is the part that takes the longest to learn. A new baker usually
presses too hard, tearing the surface, or works too gently, leaving the loaf
slack and flat. The experienced hands move in short, confident strokes that
look almost careless. They fold the dough toward the body, turn it a quarter,
fold again, and then drag it across the table so that the surface tightens
into a smooth skin. Four seconds, maybe five. Asked how they know when a loaf
is right, the bakers usually shrug and say that it simply feels right. What
they mean is that they have shaped many thousands of loaves and have kept the
ones that worked.

By six o'clock the first trays come out of the ovens, and the smell reaches the
street. The early customers are always the same: a nurse coming off the night
shift, two builders on their way to a site, a man who walks his dog and buys
one roll for himself and a smaller one for the dog. They do not see the four
hours of work behind the counter. They see bread, warm and ordinary, which is
exactly the point. Good practice disappears into the thing it makes.

The weather station on the hill above our village is nothing more than a white
wooden box on four legs, a rain gauge, and a wind vane that squeaks when the
wind swings to the north. A retired schoolteacher has read it every morning at
nine o'clock for twenty-six years. She records the highest and lowest
temperatures of the past day, the rainfall in millimeters, the direction of
the wind, and a short description of the sky. She has missed only a handful of
days, and on each of those days a neighbor read the instruments for her and
wrote their initials beside the numbers.

Her records have become quietly famous. Researchers from the university have
visited twice to copy them, because a long, careful series from a single place
is rare and valuable. The official stations nearby have moved, been rebuilt,
or changed their instruments several times, which creates small jumps in their
data that are hard to correct. The box on the hill has stayed exactly where it
is, painted the same white every spring, read at the same hour by the same
careful hand. Its numbers can be compared across decades without worrying that
the method changed underneath them.

She is proud of that consistency, but she is also strict about it. When the
old thermometer cracked eight years ago, she did not simply replace it and
carry on. She ran the new thermometer beside the old one's replacement for a
full month, wrote both readings every morning, and made a note in the margin
explaining the change. "If I don't say what changed," she told me, "then
someone will think the hill got warmer overnight." That margin note is exactly
the kind of detail that makes her records trustworthy.

Visitors sometimes ask whether the work is boring. She admits that most days
are ordinary; the sky is gray, the wind is from the west, and it rains a
little. But she says the ordinary days are the whole point. Anyone can
remember a great storm. Only a patient record can tell you that this March was
drier than the last twenty, or that frosts now end nine days earlier than they
did when she started. Those facts are built from ordinary mornings, written
down without fuss, one after another, until the pattern is strong enough to
speak for itself.

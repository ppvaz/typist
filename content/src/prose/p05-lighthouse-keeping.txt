For most of its history the lighthouse on the northern point needed a keeper
every night of the year. The lamp had to be lit at dusk, the lens had to be
cleaned of salt and soot, and the clockwork that turned the light had to be
wound every few hours so that the beam kept its steady rhythm across the
water. Ships knew the lighthouse by that rhythm: two short flashes, a pause,
and one long flash. If the pattern faltered, a captain miles offshore might
mistake it for another light and set the wrong course.

The keepers kept a log, and several of those logs survive in the small museum
at the harbor. They are not dramatic documents. Most entries follow the same
form: the time the lamp was lit, the weather, the state of the sea, the number
of times the clockwork was wound, and the time the lamp was put out in the
morning. Occasionally there is a note about a storm, a passing ship that
signaled, or a gull that broke a pane of the lantern room. Page after page,
the handwriting stays neat and the columns stay straight.

What the logs show, above all, is a kind of patient attention. The keepers
could not see the ships they were helping, and they rarely learned whether
their light had mattered on any given night. Their work was judged by its
consistency, not by its visible results. So they built habits that did not
depend on mood. They climbed the same stairs at the same times, checked the
same brass fittings in the same order, and wrote everything down, even when
nothing had happened. A blank night was still recorded as a night.

The lamp was automated long ago, and the last keeper left the point before I
was born. Yet the building still carries traces of that daily routine. The
stone steps are worn into shallow bowls where thousands of boots landed in the
same place. The handrail is polished smooth at the height of a tired hand. It
is easy to imagine someone climbing in the dark, counting the steps without
looking, knowing by feel exactly where each one would be. That knowledge was
not a talent. It was the slow deposit of repetition, laid down one night at a
time, until the body could do the work while the mind watched the sea.

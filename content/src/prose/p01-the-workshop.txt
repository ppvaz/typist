The workshop at the end of the lane was never locked. Anyone could walk in,
take a chair from the corner, and watch the old carpenter work. He did not
talk much while his hands were busy, but he did not mind an audience either.
Most visitors expected him to move quickly, the way people move in films, with
a flourish and a clean result in a single stroke. Instead he moved slowly, and
he stopped often. He would lift a plane, sight along the board, set the plane
down, and touch the wood with the flat of his palm as if he were checking a
child for fever. Only then would he make the next pass.

When someone finally asked why he paused so much, he laughed and said that
speed was the last thing a hand learns. "First the hand learns where things
are," he said. "Then it learns how hard to press. Then it learns when to stop.
Speed comes on its own, a year later, when you have stopped asking for it."
He held up a chisel with a handle worn pale where his thumb had rested for
decades. "This did not happen in a week."

His apprentices tended to fight that advice. They wanted to cut a joint in an
afternoon and see it close without a gap. They pushed, the chisel wandered,
and the joint came out loose. He never scolded them. He asked them to cut the
same joint again the next morning, and the morning after that, and to write
down what had gone wrong each time. After a month, the notebooks were more
useful than the joints. They showed which mistakes repeated and which ones had
quietly disappeared.

There is a lesson in that for any skill that lives in the hands. The first
attempts feel clumsy because the body has no map yet; every movement has to be
planned, and planning is slow. Practice does not make the planning faster so
much as it makes the planning unnecessary. The hand simply goes to the right
place, and the mind is free to think about the next thing. You cannot rush
that change, but you can make room for it: short sessions, honest notes, and a
willingness to repeat the plain exercises that nobody wants to watch.

On the wall above his bench he kept a single card with three words written in
pencil: look, feel, again. Visitors assumed it was a motto. It was really a
checklist, and he followed it every day.
